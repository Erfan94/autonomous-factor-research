# I. Introduction

Language-model agents can now read a paper’s code, write software, run it and interpret what comes out. That makes it possible to hand an agent a research programme rather than a single task. In cross-sectional equity research the natural programme is the factor search: take a catalogue of published predictors, rebuild each one on your universe, test it, and assemble the survivors into an alpha model. An agent can carry out every step of that search, and it can do so faster and more exhaustively than a person. The question this paper asks is what it takes for the result to be trustworthy.

The difficulty is that the factor search is exactly where empirical finance goes wrong. Harvey, Liu, and Zhu (2016) counted more than three hundred published predictors and argued that a t-statistic of two no longer carries its textbook meaning once that many have been tried. McLean and Pontiff (2016) showed that predictor returns fall by about a third after publication, and Hou, Xue, and Zhang (2020) found that most published anomalies do not survive a careful replication. Chen and Zimmermann (2022) answered by releasing open, reproducible code for the whole catalogue, the Open Source Asset Pricing (OSAP) project. That removed one source of error and left the search itself as the main one. The failures are procedural. The number of predictors tried is rarely written down. Thresholds, and the order in which candidates are tested, tend to settle after the results are in. The backtest code changes between the first candidate and the fortieth, and neither result says so. The holdout is looked at more than once. And a predictor that is strong on its own but redundant with the model already held is added anyway, bringing noise and turnover rather than information.

Handing the search to an agent does not remove these failures; it makes them worse. An agent can test thousands of variants in an afternoon, far more than any person, and unless something records them it will not remember in its next session what it already tried. It can change the code that measures results as easily as the code that defines a factor, and it can misread its own output, as it did in this project. A careful researcher guards against these failures through personal discipline. An agent cannot be relied on to do so, so the protection has to come from the system around it: rules and records that the agent can neither change nor forget.

This paper proposes such an environment, an autonomous research loop, and reports its first complete run. The loop rests on one division of labour: agents propose, code decides. Agents do the open-ended work: reading a reference implementation, mapping its Compustat and CRSP inputs to a vendor’s fields, writing the factor definition, auditing it, interpreting a result and writing the record. A deterministic harness does everything that must be identical across candidates: building the point-in-time universe, ranking within sector, forming deciles, hedging the long-short to the market, computing the statistics and applying the acceptance rules. Around both sits a layer of enforcement written in code, made of phase gates, commit hooks, permission tiers and cryptographic stamps, that no agent can talk its way past. The human designs the rules, supplies the data, and answers seven questions the loop may not decide alone. Everything else, from which predictor to translate next to when to fix the harness and what to write down, the loop decides and logs.

The paper makes four contributions. First, it describes the architecture of a loop in which agents run a factor search end to end without being able to change how results are measured (Section II). Second, it shows that uniform measurement and pre-registration can be made properties of the bytes rather than of anyone’s conduct. Four hashes are stamped on every result, the phases are gated in code, and nothing in the record is edited after the fact. Third, it shows how an agent loop can take a whole catalogue as its denominator: every predictor is inventoried, and every one not tested is written down with a measured reason. Fourth, it pairs the loop with a selection rule and a construction suited to automation. The composite is a blend of economic families, so near-duplicate signals cannot take whole votes, and a signal is admitted only when its information beyond every existing leg is significant. Every signal is ranked within its sector, and every long-short is hedged to the market with a beta estimated on past months only; the unhedged series and its beta are reported alongside. Signals are selected for the information they add, not for their in-sample returns. How the model would be traded is reported separately and plays no part in selection.

Within three days, the loop inventoried all {{n_osap}} predictors in the Chen and Zimmermann (2022) catalogue, ran {{n_screened}} screens over the {{n_translated}} predictors that Sharadar’s data can construct and one declared reversal, ratchet-tested the {{n_s1_pass}} that passed the standalone screen, and accepted {{n_s2_acc}}. It took a five-leg Fama-French-plus-momentum baseline to a {{v14_legs}}-leg composite in {{v14_fams}} families, and the mean rank IC rose from {{v0_ic}} to {{v14a_ic}} over 1999 to 2021. It then stopped for my approval, refreshed its data under a declared protocol and spent the reserved {{ho_n}}-month out-of-sample block once. Three results stand out. The first is that the rules held, including where they were known to be imperfect. The hedged return guard carries a disclosed bias in favour of legs that push the composite’s market beta down, and the loop reported the bias rather than repairing it with a new bar; every one of the {{n_s2_rej}} ratchet rejections fell on the information bar, and the guard never bound. The second is that the information bars accumulated a market exposure they do not measure. As the ratchet admitted low-volatility, illiquidity and profitability legs, the full-window beta of the composite’s raw long-short went from {{v0_beta}} to {{v14a_beta}}; the hedge removed that exposure on paper, and every acceptance-time headline is the hedged one. The third is that out of sample the ranking information held but the return did not. Over the out-of-sample months the composite’s IC was {{ho_ic}}, above the second-half in-window benchmark named in the expectations written before the spend, and significant at the 10% level (t-stat {{ho_ic_t}}); the hedged long-short earned {{ho_h_ret}}% a year at a Sharpe of {{ho_h_sh}} against an expected {{iw_h_sh}}, and {{hedge_term_ho}} percentage points of that return were the hedge term, the long market position a negative-beta hedge adds, earned in a rising market. The hedge was also too small: its trailing beta lagged a book whose realised beta was {{ho_beta_real}}, so the hedged series stayed partly short the market.

The number I would defend most strongly is not the IC gain but the ledger around it: {{n_s1_fail}} predictors rejected at the screen, {{n_s2_rej}} rejected at the ratchet, {{n_frontier}} excluded for a logged reason, and one holdout spent once with its expectations written first. Every rejection is logged with the same detail as every acceptance, so a reader can see what the search tried, why each candidate failed, and what the model did on data it had never seen. The mixed out-of-sample result is what the framework produced, and it is the useful result. It was not chosen, it matched what the in-window diagnostics had already printed about the beta and the regime concentration, and it could be reported without retuning anything because how it would be read was committed in advance.

The paper is organised as follows. Section II describes the framework in detail: its principles, its participants, the instruction file that drives the runner, the phases and their gates, one predictor’s path through the loop, and the mechanisms for provenance, memory, autonomy and review. Section III describes the data: the Sharadar snapshot, the predictor catalogue and the monthly universe. Section IV describes what the harness measures, including the within-sector ranks and the market hedge, and Section V sets out the selection rules. Sections VI to IX report the loop’s first complete run as a case study: the search phase by phase, the factors it selected, the construction layer, and the out-of-sample result. Section X describes the episodes in which the safeguards changed something. Section XI reads the result, Section XII states the limitations, and Section XIII says which parts of the design transfer. The appendices hold the full ledgers: every ratchet rung, every Stage 1 rejection, every predictor not tested and why, the agents’ division of labour, the reproduction proofs, and a description of each accepted leg.

# II. The Framework

## A. Design Principles

Six principles shaped every component of the loop. Each answers one of the procedural failures above, and each is enforced by something a reader can inspect.

**Agents propose, code decides.** An agent may decide what to test. It may never decide how the test is measured. Factor files read data only through a month context that the harness supplies, and they may not contain a universe filter, a date, a rebalance rule, a sector ranking, a hedge or any statistic. A factor that needs different machinery has to extend the harness, which changes the harness hash and makes the change visible on every later result.

**Provenance is a property of the bytes.** Every result carries hashes of the code, the configuration, the composite and the data that produced it, and a result whose hashes do not match the repository is refused. Nobody has to be trusted to have run the right thing.

**Every rule is written before it is needed.** The bars, the test order, the Newey-West lag, the out-of-sample boundary, the way the long-short is ranked and hedged, and the expectations against which the out-of-sample block would be read were fixed before the scores they govern existed. Where the loop had to interpret a rule during the search, it logged the ruling and never introduced a new rule.

**The denominator is the whole catalogue.** The loop does not propose its own candidates. It inventories every predictor in a public catalogue, here the Open Source Asset Pricing catalogue of Chen and Zimmermann (2022), and accounts for each one, either with a test result or with a measured reason why it could not be tested.

**Autonomy has a closed boundary.** The loop decides everything except a fixed list of seven questions. The list is short enough that the loop can run for days without a human. It is closed, so the loop cannot widen its own authority.

**Everything is written down, rejections included.** Every run, verdict, ruling and correction goes into an append-only record in the same detail, so the rejection table is as complete as the acceptance table.

## B. Architecture and Participants

Figure 1 shows the loop’s participants and the layers that constrain them. The orchestration layer decides what to do next. The runner, a language model working from a standing instruction file, drives the search. It consults a stronger advisor model at fixed points and puts seven kinds of questions to the human. The sub-agent layer does the specialised work that needs judgement. The measurement layer is a deterministic Python harness that computes every statistic and applies every bar. The record layer holds everything the loop has done. Beneath all four, a layer of scripts, hooks and permission rules enforces the boundaries in code.

Each sub-agent is defined by its own instruction file, which states its job, its inputs, what it must report and what it must never do, together with a list of the tools it may use. The adversarial reviewer, for example, can read and search files but cannot write or run anything. Its instructions tell it to assume the code is wrong until verified and to treat an unusually strong result as a reason for more suspicion. The translator writes one factor file and may not put a universe filter, a date or a statistic into it. Nor may it assign the factor a family, which happens later and elsewhere. The evaluator checks provenance before anything else and refuses to write a record for a result whose stamps disagree with the repository. Appendix D gives the full division of labour.

## C. The Standing Instruction File

The runner has no memory between sessions and no goal of its own. Both come from a standing instruction file in the repository’s root, which the runner loads at the start of every session. The file is short, a few pages, and it works as the program the runner executes. It states the goal: a multi-factor model, with every candidate recorded. It states the one constraint that shapes everything, that every test runs on the same stamped bytes. It gives the pre-registered rules in summary, including the construction choices, the seven stop-and-ask questions, the five phases and what closes each, a list of hard rules, the table of records the loop must keep, and the commands that run each step.

Two instructions in the file do most of the work of making the loop autonomous. The first is “a turn ends with a run executed and a decision logged, not with a question”. When the runner is about to ask the human something that the rules already answer, it is told to apply the rule, say in one sentence which rule it applied, and continue. The second fixes the shape of every reply. Each one ends with a table of every composite version and its headline statistics, a line saying how many candidates have been accepted, screened and inventoried and which phase the search is in, and one sentence saying what is running, what the human owes, or what is blocked. A human who looks in at any moment can see the state of the search without reading the transcript.

The file is written in plain language, and plain language is not enforcement. Every rule in it that can be checked by a script is also checked by one. The file says Stage 2 may not start before the families are declared, and a records checker refuses the run. It says the holdout is spent once, and the harness’s validation flags any result block that reaches 2022 as an out-of-sample breach, while the permission layer asks the human before either holdout flag can be passed. The instruction file tells the runner what to do. The code makes sure the important parts were done.

The instruction file is also the part of the framework that a reader would change to point the loop at a different problem. The catalogue, the data vendor, the bars, the construction and the phases are all named there. The mechanisms underneath, the stamps, the gates, the hooks and the record, do not depend on any of them.

## D. The Phase Machine

The search runs in five phases after a one-time setup, and no phase starts before the previous one closes (Table II and Figure 2). The order is itself a safeguard. The loop inventories everything before it screens anything, so it cannot choose what to examine after seeing results. It screens every constructible predictor before it assigns any family, and it assigns every family and declares the test order before any Stage 2 number exists, so neither the families nor the order can be fitted to marginal results.

## E. The Inner Cycle

Within a phase, the runner repeats the same cycle on every turn. It reads the current state and finishes any open item before starting a new one. It declares the next batch in the event log, with its members and the stamps it will run under. It runs the harness. When the run finishes, it hands the result to the evaluator, which checks the four stamps against the repository, applies the bars, confirms that its own verdicts equal the ones the harness stamped, and writes one registry row per candidate along with the events that record the decision. Before committing a ladder’s acceptances the runner consults the advisor, and the evaluator then materialises each new version: it moves the factor file into the accepted set, edits the composite, runs a fresh baseline and a construction run for the new version, and appends a block to the model manifest. The runner then commits the step with the four stamps in the commit message, adds one annotated tag per new version, and updates the state file. Only then does the runner reply, and the reply ends with the status blocks described above. Figure 3 draws the cycle. A rejection takes the short path: the rows are written, the composite is unchanged and the step is committed.

!figure cycle

The cycle is sequential on purpose. In the ratchet, rung i+1’s base depends on rung i’s verdict, and every accepted version must be measured and tagged before the next ladder is cut. Parallelism is used only where it cannot change a result: the inventory ran several fetches at a time, because a specification depends on nothing but its own reference code.

## F. Example: One Predictor through the Loop

PctAcc, the percent accruals of Hafzalla, Lundholm, and Van Winkle (2011), went first through the ratchet, and following it shows how the parts fit together.

The fetcher wrote its specification from the reference code at the pinned commit. The construction is income before extraordinary items minus operating cash flow, scaled by the absolute value of income, with low accruals attractive. The catalogue’s code has a balance-sheet fallback for firms without a cash-flow statement that needs income taxes payable, which Sharadar does not publish; the loop logged a decision not to reproduce that branch, at a measured cost of about 0.3% of the universe, rather than approximate it. The translator wrote the factor file, and preflight passed. The reviewer’s audit of its batch raised the one critical finding of the whole inventory, against a neighbour: PriceDelayRsq, a return-window signal in the same batch, had declared itself exempt from the history gate that every return-window factor must carry. It was fixed and re-preflighted before any screen.

PctAcc was screened in the seventh batch of twelve (run 009). It passed every bar, with a mean IC of {{s1ic_pctacc}} and a Newey-West t-stat of {{s1t_pctacc}}, positive in both halves ({{pctacc_h1}} and {{pctacc_h2}}), a raw long-short of {{pctacc_raw}}% a year and coverage of {{pctacc_cov}}%. The evaluator also logged what the bars do not test: the IC level cleared its bar by a thin margin, the second half was under half the first, and the information sat in the mid and small tiers. It was then assigned to the investment family, because the decision that fixed the label-to-family map absorbed the catalogue’s accruals label into investment. The order of the ratchet was declared at the same time, and PctAcc came first, because its Stage 1 t-stat was the highest of the {{n_s1_pass}} passers.

It was the first rung of the first ladder (run 012), tested against the five-leg baseline. Its residual IC t-stat was {{rt_pctacc}} against a bar of 2.0, and its guard t-stat was {{gt_pctacc}} against a floor of −2.0, so it was accepted. The composite’s IC rose with a paired t-stat of {{pctacc_dic_t}} while its long-short barely moved, and the evaluator recorded why: the information showed in the ranking, not in the spread. The evaluator re-derived the verdict from the block, and the runner consulted the advisor before committing. The version was then materialised as v1: the file moved to the accepted set, the composite gained a sixth leg, the fresh baseline (run 013) reproduced the ladder’s arm on every compared field to six places, and the construction run (run 014) was added to the manifest. The tag is where the trail shows a flaw. A chained command whose commit failed on a stale path still ran its tag step, so v1-add-PctAcc annotates the commit before v1. The loop logged the mistake, did not move the tag, because tags are never moved, and left the decision to me. From specification to version took less than a day, and every step left a timestamped event, a file and a commit that a reader can check.

## G. Provenance and Immutability

Four hashes go on every result block: HARNESS_SHA over the harness code, CONFIG_SHA over the pre-registration file, COMPOSITE_SHA over the composite definition together with its accepted legs, and DATA_SHA over the manifest of the frozen data snapshot. The construction layer adds a fifth, LAYER_SHA, over its parameter file. The evaluator’s first step on any run is to compare the block’s stamps with the repository’s. On a mismatch it logs the fact and writes nothing, so a number produced by other code, other rules or other data cannot enter the record. The harness also stamps its own verdict into each block, and the evaluator re-derives every verdict from the block’s raw statistics. A disagreement is treated as a harness defect, not as a rounding difference.

Across all {{n_runs_started}} recorded runs, CONFIG_SHA and the universe hash each took exactly one value, so the pre-registration never moved between the first baseline and the holdout. DATA_SHA took two: the first pull, on which every decision of the search was taken, and the refresh that the holdout protocol required, which I approved as a stop-and-ask question. HARNESS_SHA moved {{n_harness_moves}} times: once at bootstrap before any run, twice for the construction layer and the fixes its review asked for, and twice for diagnostics added after the ratchet closed. None of the moves fell between the first screen and the close of the ratchet, and every harness that later produced a number was first shown to reproduce the live composite on every compared field (Appendix E). The one run started under a harness that was superseded before it finished is in the record as an aborted run.

Git hooks make the record hard to rewrite. The pre-commit hook refuses snapshot bytes, anything that looks like a credential or an API key, any change to the harness, a factor or the configuration that does not pass the test suite, and a results file that is still being written. It also refuses an edit to a results file that has already been committed. The commit-message hook requires a type prefix and the four stamps. Each composite version gets one annotated tag, which is never moved, and mistakes are undone with a new commit rather than by rewriting history. The permission layer asks the human before a hard reset.

## H. Memory and Continuity

A language model forgets everything between sessions, and a search of this size runs across many sessions. The loop therefore keeps its memory in files, and each file has one job. A state file of at most 25 lines holds the current state only: the phase, the live composite and its stamps, the counters, the open items and the next step. Its history goes to a separate file, so the state stays short enough to read in full at the start of every session. The event log is append-only and holds numbers and identifiers rather than prose. It had {{n_events}} events when the search closed, and every decision in this paper can be traced to one of them. A registry holds one row per candidate with its bars and verdict, and an index gives one line per candidate. The model manifest holds one block per composite version. A decisions file records every design decision with its date, owner and evidence, and a journal records each step with what it ran and what it cost.

The records checker ties these together. It rebuilds the index from the registry, reconciles the catalogue against candidate files, registry rows and logged exclusions, and prints the predictors still open. It reports drift when a phase gate is violated, when a completed run is unevaluated, or when the proof that the snapshot still matches the vendor’s API is missing, stale or taken on a different data hash. A fresh session starts by reading the state file and running the checker, and from there it can resume in the middle of a phase. Operational lessons that are not rules of the search are kept as short notes that later sessions load automatically.

## I. The Autonomy Boundary

The runner stops for a human on seven questions only: spending the out-of-sample block; anything that moves CONFIG_SHA; anything that moves DATA_SHA; moving the pinned reference-code commit; declaring the search finished; a rule contradicting itself, or a result implying that logged rows are wrong; and opening a tenth family. Everything else it decides, logs the reasoning, and carries out.

The permission layer backs the list with a second line of defence. Commands the loop needs are allowed without asking: running the harness, preflight and the record scripts, running the tests, writing under the research, factor, harness, documentation and source directories, and committing and tagging. Actions that correspond to the stop-and-ask list require the human’s approval each time: editing the pre-registration file or the snapshot manifest, downloading data, passing either holdout flag, or resetting history. Some actions are denied outright. These are reading the file that holds the API key, deleting the data, and reading any directory outside the project, so that nothing but this search’s own measurements can inform a verdict.

My own role in the run was limited to four things. First, I supplied the data: a Sharadar licence and the API key that pulled the snapshot. Second, I answered the stop-and-ask questions. Five of the seven arose, and I resolved them in three decisions. Before any run, the loop found that its decisions file contradicted itself on which bars the hedge should reach (question 6); I resolved it with a change to the configuration (question 2), under which the Stage 1 spread bar reads the raw long-short and the Stage 2 guard the hedged one (ruling D11). Later I approved the snapshot refresh that the holdout protocol required (question 3), and at the end I confirmed that the search was finished and authorised the holdout spend (questions 5 and 1). Third, I gave one authorisation outside the list: when the session’s permission layer refused a sub-agent the file move and composite edit for one acceptance, the loop waited rather than routing around the refusal, and I authorised the runner to make those moves itself. Fourth, I occasionally steered the run, for example by asking why some runs were slow. I wrote the design rules and fixed them before the loop started, in a bootstrap session that set up the repository and measured the baseline. During the run I did not edit any record by hand; that is a statement rather than something the records can prove.

## J. The Advisor

The runner is built to keep moving, and an agent that keeps moving can rationalise its way past a problem. The advisor is the counterweight. It is a stronger model that sees the runner’s whole transcript, every tool call and every result, and is asked what is wrong with the current plan. The instruction file requires a consultation at every phase boundary, before every acceptance is committed, before any stop-and-ask question is raised, and whenever a result looks too good, such as a single-factor t-stat above about 6 or a residual share near 100%. Advice that changes a decision is logged.

The event log records {{n_advisor}} consultations explicitly: before committing each of the four ladders that produced acceptances, at the boundary between the ratchet and the construction phase, and on the verdict that the snapshot refresh could not have moved any Stage 2 decision. The instruction file asks for more than that, and consultations that were not logged cannot be verified from the record, so I count only these. The record shows that the consultations happened, not what the advisor said. The decisions logged beside them set working rules rather than reversing verdicts: acceptances are applied one version at a time in ladder order, each version’s baseline must reproduce its rung to six places or the sequence stops, and the construction phase reads the Stage 3 run of the final composite rather than a new one.

Two more model choices belong on the record. The runner was Claude Opus (version 5.5 in the journal), operating in the Claude Code desktop application, after a bootstrap session on Claude Fable 5.1; the advisor was Claude Fable. The fetcher, field checker and translator are defined on Claude Sonnet, and the reviewer and evaluator run on the runner’s model. None of these choices affects a number. The harness is the same Python code whichever model invokes it, and the evaluator re-derives every verdict from the stamped block rather than from any model’s reading of it.

## K. Why the Rules Take This Form

Most rules in this paper answer a specific way an automated factor search can go wrong.

Stage 2 accepts on information and prints spanning as a diagnostic. A return-based spanning test selects on portfolio return, which contradicts the aim of selecting on information, and it can reject a candidate that raises the composite’s own IC. No transaction cost is charged anywhere in the search: a flat cost charged on standalone decile portfolios would penalise high-turnover signals, although a leg in a blend contributes only a fraction of its standalone turnover, so turnover is reported instead. Phase A inventories the whole catalogue, because a frontier built from whichever files happen to have been translated leaves predictors unexamined without anyone deciding to exclude them. The out-of-sample block is {{ho_n}} months long, because a tail of a few months has no power. The rule against moving a stamp while a run is unevaluated covers every stamp, because a run is stranded whichever stamp moves. And the composite is a family blend with a residual-information bar, because a paired composite-IC bar is underpowered for one leg added to a blend of about nine legs, and an equal-weight blend lets near-duplicates take whole votes.

The construction follows the same logic. A ratchet that accepts on information and guards only the raw long-short can accumulate a large market beta that none of its rules measures, and a construction layer that is neutral to sector and to dollars but not to beta leaves that exposure in the book. This search therefore ranks every signal within its sector, hedges every long-short with a trailing beta, prints the beta and a date-free ex-regime Sharpe on every block, and constrains the construction layer’s beta.

These choices reflect prior knowledge of how factor searches behave over the same sample period, including the years reserved here as the holdout. The holdout is therefore a clean test of the selection made inside this search but not an unbiased test of the design choices themselves, and the decisions file says so (D2). Every candidate was judged on this snapshot alone, and every field mapping was verified on it before use. Section XII returns to the design’s dependence on the window.

# III. Data

The loop’s results rest on three inputs: the vendor data, the catalogue of predictors the candidates come from, and the monthly universe drawn from the data. This section describes each, and Table III summarises the sample.

## A. The Sharadar Snapshot

The data are this project’s own pull of {{n_sharadar_tables}} Sharadar tables with full history, including {{sep_rows_m}} million daily price rows, frozen by a manifest whose hash is DATA_SHA. A live check at the start of every run proves that the frozen bytes still match the API’s schema and history. Fundamentals are read from Sharadar’s as-reported trailing-twelve-month dimension, taking the most recent filing as of the signal date and allowing it to be at most fifteen months old; a factor that needs quarterly or annual statements declares the override in its file, and the harness prints every override on the run. Where a Compustat line has no Sharadar equivalent, the factor file declares the gap rather than estimating around it. The data are licensed from Sharadar through Nasdaq Data Link and may not be redistributed, so the repository holds the manifest and the code that reads the tables, not the tables themselves.

The snapshot was recorded twice. The first pull was full-history, so it already held the months reserved as the holdout: the holdout was isolated by rule, not by absent bytes. Every result block of every decision run has an evaluation end of 31 December 2021, and a block that reaches 2022 is flagged as an out-of-sample breach. # IV. Methodology: What the Harness Measures

This section and the next describe the harness: the fixed measurement that every candidate faces and that no agent can change without the change showing on every later result. Two parts of it deserve particular attention, the within-sector ranks and the market hedge; both were fixed before any candidate was screened.

## A. Signals, Ranks and Forward Returns

A signal is observed at the prior business month-end and held for the following month. Within each month it is winsorised at the 1st and 99th percentiles and converted to a percentile rank within the name’s sector, oriented so that a high rank is attractive according to the catalogue’s published sign. A sector-month with fewer than {{min_names_group}} scored names falls back to the cross-section rank. Ranking within sector means every signal compares a firm with its peers: a value signal does not become a bet on cheap industries, and the composite does not load on sectors by construction. The sector is Sharadar’s current classification applied to every month, a look-ahead the decisions file states (D3). The forward return runs from the signal-date close to the next business month-end, using Sharadar’s adjusted close. Delistings follow Shumway’s (1997) convention by reason: a name that leaves for a performance reason (bankruptcy, a regulatory delisting) is assigned a return of −30% for its final partial month, and one that leaves through a merger or voluntary delisting earns its last observed price. Performance delistings are rare in this universe, {{fwd_perf}} name-months out of {{u_firm_months}} in the final in-window run against {{fwd_merger}} merger delistings, so the convention is rarely triggered.

## B. The Information Coefficient and Its Newey-West t-Statistic

Every selection decision in this paper rests on one statistic, the monthly rank information coefficient, and on a Newey-West t-statistic of its mean, so it is worth being precise about both.

In month t the IC is the Spearman rank correlation, across all names with both a score and a forward return that month, between the signal’s within-sector percentile rank and the forward return. It is computed on the continuous score, not on decile membership, so that within-decile ordering counts. A month needs at least ten scored names to produce an IC. Over the decision window this gives a series of up to {{iw_n}} monthly values (fewer for a signal with a history gate). The mean of that series is the mean IC reported everywhere in this paper; the ICIR is the mean divided by the standard deviation of the series.

Monthly IC series are autocorrelated, so the t-statistic of the mean uses the Newey and West (1987) long-run variance with a Bartlett kernel. With demeaned values \(e_t\), \(T\) months and \(L\) lags, the long-run variance is

!eq \mathrm{LRV} = \frac{1}{T} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} e_t^2 + 2 \sum_{l=1}^{L} \left( 1 - \frac{l}{L+1} \right) \sum_{t=l+1}^{T} e_t \, e_{t-l} \right] ,

!noindent and the t-statistic is the mean IC divided by the square root of \(LRV/T\). The lag is registered in the configuration as \(L\) = {{nw_lags}} and is the same for every t-statistic in the search: the IC series, the long-short return series, and every paired difference. It was fixed before the first screen.

The Stage 2 residual IC is built the same way on a transformed score. In each month the candidate’s percentile rank is mapped to a normal score, and so is each current leg’s; the candidate’s normal score is regressed, with an intercept, on the legs’ normal scores (a name that lacks a leg is set to that leg’s cross-sectional mean of zero); and the residual’s Spearman correlation with the forward return is that month’s residual IC. The series’ mean and Newey-West t-stat follow as above. The residual IC measures the information the candidate carries that no linear combination of the existing legs carries, and it does not depend on how the legs are weighted. The residual share reported on every rung is the mean residual IC divided by the candidate’s own mean IC.

## C. The Long-Short and the Market Hedge

The raw long-short return of a signal is the equal-weight return of its top decile minus its bottom decile, formed each month on the same ranks, gross of any cost. A decile spread is not market-neutral. Signals that favour low-volatility, illiquid or profitable firms hold a long side with less market exposure than the short side, so their raw spread is short the market, and a ratchet that accepts on information can accumulate that exposure leg by leg without any bar seeing it. The harness therefore reports every long-short hedged to the market (D4):

!eq \mathrm{LS}_t = \left( R_{D10,t} - R_{D1,t} \right) - \beta_t \, M_t ,

!noindent where \(M_t\) is the universe’s own capitalisation-weighted total return and \(β_t\) is the slope of the raw spread on \(M\) estimated over months \(t\)−{{beta_window}} to \(t\)−1 only, set to zero before {{beta_min}} months of history exist. The beta is ex ante: no month’s hedge uses that month’s return. Both series are annualised as the monthly mean times twelve, so the difference between them is exactly −12 times the mean of \(β_t\)·\(M_t\). This paper calls that difference the hedge term. For a book that is short the market (\(β_t < 0\)) in a rising market the hedge term is positive: the hedge adds a long market position to a net-short spread.

The hedge reaches the bars in exactly one place. The Stage 1 positive-spread bar reads the raw spread, and the Stage 2 guard reads the hedged family blend (ruling D11, Section V). Every headline in the paper is hedged, and every block prints beside it the raw series, the ex-ante beta averaged over the window, the full-window beta of the raw spread, and two diagnostics that never decide (D5). The first is a date-free ex-regime Sharpe: the hedged Sharpe after removing the {{top_years_k}} best calendar years of the long-short, whichever they are. The second splits the months by the sign of the market’s trailing {{mkt_state_lb}}-month return into a bear and a bull Sharpe.

Two properties of the hedge were known, disclosed and left in place, because changing the hedge would move the pre-registration. First, \(M\) is a total return, so a negative-beta book is credited |\(β_t\)| times the risk-free rate as if it were return. After the refresh added the Treasury bill rate, the harness reports beside every hedged series an excess-of-rf diagnostic, \(LS_t\) + \(β_t\)·\(rf_t\) with \(rf\) the three-month bill rate of the holding month, and the difference, the rf credit, in percentage points a year; it is a diagnostic and decides nothing. Second, the guard inherits the hedge term: a candidate that pushes the blend’s ex-ante beta down is credited through the hedge whatever its information, so for a negative-beta leg the guard is close to non-binding and the residual IC is the operative gate (decision hedge_guard_negative_beta_property, logged during the ratchet).

The paired change in long-short return that the Stage 2 guard reads is the month-by-month difference between the hedged family blend’s long-short return with the candidate and without it, and its t-statistic is the Newey-West t-stat of that difference series. Sharpe ratios are annualised from monthly values; long-short t-statistics are Newey-West as above.

## D. The Family Blend

A flat equal-weight blend lets near-duplicates take whole votes: three low-volatility signals would carry three times the weight of one. The composite here is a two-level blend. Every Stage 1 passer is assigned, by its economic definition, to one of fewer than ten families, and the composite score is the equal-weight mean of family scores, each family score being the equal-weight mean of its members’ within-sector percentile ranks. Both means are taken over the legs a name actually has, so a missing leg neither drops the name nor pulls it to the middle. A new leg therefore dilutes only its own family. Families are assigned after a candidate’s screen and before any Stage 2 number exists, with a timestamp, and are never changed afterwards. The five seed families are fixed by the baseline: size, value, profitability, investment and momentum. The reference for assignment is the catalogue’s own economic category, read against the construction.

!table families

The baseline, v0, is the four stock-level characteristics behind the Fama-French five-factor model (the market factor has no cross-sectional counterpart) plus twelve-month momentum, as rank signals with one family each: small size, high book-to-market, high operating profitability, low asset growth, and past winners with the most recent month skipped. It was measured and tagged before any candidate was screened.

# V. The Selection Rules

Every parameter in Table V is fixed in a single configuration file and hashed into CONFIG_SHA. Changing any of them is a re-baseline, which only the human can authorise.

!table harness

## Stage 1, the Standalone Screen

Stage 1 is a screen. Its only job is to stop weak or broken factors from consuming a ratchet run, and passing it earns a family assignment and a Stage 2 rung, nothing more. A candidate passes when all of the following hold on the decision window: a mean monthly IC of at least 0.010; a Newey-West IC t-statistic of at least 2.50; a positive mean IC in each half of the window; a positive gross annual return on the raw decile-10-minus-decile-1 spread; coverage of at least 40% of universe name-months; and at least 30 names per decile on average. The spread bar reads the raw series on purpose: a hedged spread bar would pass a signal whose own spread loses money but whose short-market tilt earns the hedge term. A candidate whose published sign fails but whose reversed sign passes is treated as a second hypothesis: it must clear an absolute t-stat of 2.74 together with every other bar, as a separately declared file.

## Stage 2, the Ratchet

Stage 2 asks whether the candidate adds information to the model that already exists. Two bars must both hold. The residual IC bar requires the Newey-West t-stat of the candidate’s residual IC, as defined in Section IV.B, to be strictly greater than 2.0. The guard requires the Newey-West t-stat of the paired change in the hedged family blend’s gross long-short return, with the candidate against without, to be at least −2.0, so that a signal which adds information but drags the blend’s market-neutral return significantly is caught. Everything else printed on a rung, the paired composite change in IC, the spanning alpha of the candidate’s long-short on the composite’s, its R², the change in Sharpe and drawdown, the raw and hedged changes in return and the change in beta, is a diagnostic. Diagnostics are reported on every rung and never decide.

The test is deliberately an information test rather than a return test. One leg added to a many-family blend moves the blend by little, so a paired test of such a change is underpowered, and a return-based spanning bar can reject candidates that raise the composite’s own IC significantly (Section II.K).

!table bars

## Order, Ladders and Re-Derivation

Candidates are screened twelve at a time in alphabetical order of their catalogue acronym, never by any number. Every Stage 1 passer gets a Stage 2 rung. The order of the rungs is descending Stage 1 t-statistic over all passers, declared once when the family phase closes and written to a file before any Stage 2 number exists; the strongest signal in a family is thereby its representative, and weaker variants must add information beyond it. Ladders of at most five rungs are cut from that list in order, and rung i faces the composite as it stands, the base plus every earlier rung that passed. Two rules are enforced by code: the order is never re-sorted on a Stage 2 result, and HARNESS_SHA never moves inside a declared ladder.

Because rung i+1’s base depends on rung i’s verdict, the harness applies both bars itself and stamps its decision into each result block. That is not a transfer of the decision. The evaluator re-derives every verdict from the block’s raw statistics, and a disagreement is treated as a harness defect rather than a rounding difference. Across all {{n_s2}} rungs the evaluator re-derived every verdict and none was overturned. Each accepted version was then materialised one at a time: its file moved, the composite edited, a fresh baseline and a Stage 3 construction run, a manifest block appended, one annotated git tag. Every version’s own baseline equals the ratchet arm that accepted it on {{repro_fields}} compared fields to six places (Appendix E).

## Rules Committed in Advance, and Rulings Made inside the Loop

The rules in Table VII were settled before they were needed, so that applying one is not a fresh choice made while looking at a result one would like to accept. The one ruling that changed how a bar is read, D11, was made at bootstrap before any run, as a stop-and-ask question that I answered. The others were made by the runner inside the loop as cases arose and are logged as decision events: how a family partition maps catalogue labels to families, when a qualified reversal need not be screened, how the guard’s hedge bias is disclosed, and which bar a multi-bar failure is attributed to. Each was logged after the case that raised it and none introduced a new rule. I say so plainly because the honest claim is that the bars predate the screen and the readings of them do not.

## Closing the Search, and What It Cost

The stopping rule was exhaustion of the declared order. The ratchet ran until every Stage 1 passer had a Stage 2 row, which happened at the {{n_s2}}th rung. The search then closed the ratchet, built the construction layer on the frozen composite (Section VIII), asked for and received the snapshot refresh, reproduced the in-window composite on the new bytes, wrote its expectations for the holdout (Section IX.A), and asked me to declare the search finished and to authorise the holdout spend, which I did.

The harness runs took {{wall_hours}} hours of compute across {{n_runs_completed}} completed runs: {{phaseB_h}} hours of screening, {{phaseD_h}} of ladders with their baselines and construction runs, and the rest in the construction phase, the refresh and the spend. # VIII. Construction

Stage 3 of the search reports five fixed construction variants on the accepted composite, gross and hedged, for every version: equal-rank deciles, tier-neutral ranking, ICIR-weighted legs, a buffered book and a volatility-targeted book. On v14 (run 043, acceptance-time bytes) the equal-rank deciles had the highest Sharpe, 0.983; the buffered book cut long-side turnover from 58.1 to 38.5% a month at a Sharpe of 0.954; tier-neutral ranking (0.858), ICIR weights (0.902) and volatility targeting (0.901) did no better. The ICIR-weighted book’s raw beta was −0.93, the most negative of the five. None of these is a gate.

After the ratchet closed, a separate phase built the book an institutional desk would trade. The layer judges nothing: no leg is added, dropped or reweighted by any number it produces, and its parameters were frozen in their own file, stamped as a fifth hash, before its first number. Its alpha is the composite’s normal score times the name’s predicted volatility. Its gross exposure is 2.0 × clip(\(IC_t\) / 0.02, 0, 1), where \(IC_t\) is the composite’s trailing 36-month rank IC. The risk model has twelve sector groups plus size and trailing volatility, with exponentially weighted covariances. The decisions file required three changes before the layer’s first number (D7). The book is neutral to market beta as well as to sector: the optimiser’s constraint matrix stacks the sector dummies and each name’s trailing 36-month beta on the same market return the hedge uses, so the target book has zero ex-ante beta. The half-spread comes from a Corwin-Schultz series that the harness builds from daily highs and lows, not from any composite leg. And the regime cuts the layer reports are the composite’s three best years by the D5 rule, not years chosen by eye. The optimiser is closed-form, followed by name caps, a no-trade buffer and a participation cap of five percent of 21 days’ volume per trade. Costs are half the measured spread, a square-root impact term, and 25 basis points a year of borrow. The book is reported at 0.1, 1 and 5 billion dollars from January 2001. The adversarial reviewer’s audit of the implementation asked for fixes first: one major finding in the design notes, which was removed; one medium, the first declared ex-year preceding the book; and several low ones, all fixed before the real run.

# X. What the Safeguards Caught

A framework is only worth describing if its mechanisms changed something. The event log holds {{n_events}} events, among them {{cnt_spec_written}} specifications written, {{cnt_factor_evaluated}} factor evaluations, {{n_alpha_reviews}} adversarial reviews, {{n_process_findings}} process findings, {{n_finding_corrected}} corrections of earlier findings and {{n_harness_moves}} harness moves. The episodes below show the mechanisms doing their work. In each, the thing that caught the problem was a sub-agent, a script or a rule, not the runner’s own care, and the last ones show the loop catching errors in its own reporting.

The adversarial reviewer caught defects before any number existed. Its audits of the translated batches found the one critical issue of the inventory, a return-window signal that had declared itself exempt from the history gate, and a dozen major ones, among them a book-to-market variant that paired book equity and market equity up to seventeen months apart, an enterprise-value floor whose logic was inverted, and a debt gate missing from five signals that read debt. Each was fixed before the screen. The translator, for its part, found a sign convention in Sharadar’s discontinued-operations field and recorded it as a known trap in the field map. The market series that three predictors need (the Pástor and Stambaugh (2003) liquidity innovation, the Kelly and Jiang (2014) tail factor, and the trend factor’s moving-average coefficients) were rebuilt inside the harness from the snapshot, on past data only.

The reviewer also enforced the boundary of the evidence. Its audit of the construction layer found that a passage in the design document, and a clause in the decisions file, cited results that this search had not produced, although only the search’s own measurements may inform it. The passage was removed, the decision was annotated with a dated note rather than rewritten, and a sweep for such text had already been run at the close of the ratchet. In all, {{n_leak_findings}} process findings concern such text, and each is logged.

A chained command did more than it should. The commit that should have recorded v1 failed on a stale path, but the tag command chained after it still ran, so the tag v1-add-PctAcc annotates the commit before v1. The loop logged it and did not move the tag, because tags are never moved; whether to delete and recreate it is my decision, and it is listed as open in the manifest. The fix written into the record is to tag only after the repository shows the version commit.

A permission refusal was not routed around. When the session’s permission layer refused a sub-agent the file move and composite edit for v12, the runner did not perform the refused action itself by another route. It logged the refusal and waited, and the move happened only after I authorised the runner to make it.

A run was stopped rather than evaluated. The construction layer’s first harness was superseded by the review fixes while a Stage 3 run under it was writing. The run was stopped before it finished and logged as aborted with no result, its partial log was kept out of the record, and the fixed harness was proved to reproduce the live composite before it produced a number.

The loop misread its own results, and later checks against the records corrected it. A batch note said IdioVol3F’s annual IC correlated 0.97 with MaxRet’s; a correction event, reading the registry, put it at 0.94 (0.97 is IdioVolAHT’s). A summary of the layer run said its return per unit of turnover was flat and that its cost ratio did not depend on leverage; a correction event showed both were wrong. In drafting the record of the search it gave a runtime for the screening phase that disagreed with the runs’ own timings, and a count of inventory reviews that disagreed with the event log. All {{n_finding_corrected}} corrections are appended to the log rather than edited in, and the record also shows where its own timestamps were estimated rather than read from the clock (rows {{ts_est_rows}}).

The protocol had a gap that only the spend revealed. The holdout protocol asked for the Stage 3 variants to be read from their holdout cuts, and the Stage 3 code path never emitted them; nothing tested that before the spend. The gap is logged and disclosed in Section IX.D rather than repaired by a second look. The fix belongs to the next pre-registration: every holdout-reading run should be tested for its cuts before the spend.

The text of this paper was checked in the same way as the search results. A read-only sub-agent traced each number to the source file that produced it and reviewed how each figure is framed. Every headline figure matched its source. The framing review fixed three conventions that the paper follows throughout: the hedged out-of-sample return is always reported together with the share that comes from the hedge term, the direction of that term is stated precisely, and the construction layer is described as negative net only for the declared row, which is the one case where it is. Each finding of the review is logged in the event record.

# XI. Reading the Result

The composite’s information was real, and much of it carried out of sample. In-window its IC averaged {{iw_ic}}, stronger in the first half ({{iw_ic_h1}}) than in the second ({{iw_ic_h2}}). Out of sample it was {{ho_ic}}, slightly above the second-half level, so the model held the strength it had shown most recently. As is typical of factor returns, the holdout IC varied from year to year. The ex-regime Sharpe, reported as a diagnostic on every acceptance row, gave a more realistic picture of that variation than the headline IC. A natural extension of the Stage 1 screen, which required a positive IC in both halves of the window, would be a test of how evenly the information is spread across years.

The information bar did what it was designed to do, and its one side effect is visible throughout the record. Residual IC does not depend on how the portfolio is built, so portfolio choices cannot game it, and it admitted signals that sharpened how well the composite ranked stocks within sectors. Several of these signals, in volatility, illiquidity, profitability and issuance, have a calmer long side than short side. As legs were added, the raw spread therefore leaned short the market, with its beta moving from {{v0_beta}} at v0 to {{v14a_beta}} at v14. The market hedge was built for exactly this, and in-window it worked: the ex-ante and realised betas agreed. Out of sample the realised beta deepened to {{ho_beta_real}} in a rising market. A trailing estimate naturally lagged it, so the hedge covered only part of the exposure, and the total-return market proxy credited the short book with interest it did not earn. Both effects are measurable, and each points to a simple refinement. The beta rows are printed on every rung, the guard’s reading of the hedged blend is a logged decision, and the risk-free credit is a printed diagnostic. A reader who prefers a beta guard or a headline in excess of the risk-free rate can see exactly which rungs and numbers it would change.

The pattern of a positive IC alongside a weak long-short appears at three scales. At Stage 1, the volatility passers had IC t-statistics near three and raw long-short Sharpes near zero. In the ratchet, MaxRet raised the IC with a significant residual while cutting the raw Sharpe. Out of sample, the composite kept a positive mean IC while its raw spread earned {{ho_raw_ret}}% a year and its top nine deciles were flat. An IC averages rank agreement over all names and months; a decile long-short is a bet on the extremes. That is a reading, and it was not tested on the holdout.

Economic and empirical families can diverge. Families were assigned by the catalogue’s economic label, before any Stage 2 number. zerotrade6M is labelled liquidity and opened the liquidity family at a full ninth of the composite. Empirically its annual IC series moves 0.96 with IdioVol3F’s and 0.91 with MaxRet’s, and its raw spread has a beta of {{beta1_zerotrade6m}}: in this universe a low-turnover sort is close to a low-volatility sort. Its admission therefore doubled the composite’s weight on what behaves like one exposure. Families are fixed after Phase C by design, so it stayed. A family structure fitted to the correlation matrix would probably have put it with the volatility legs. That would be a legitimate alternative design, but it is a different pre-registration.

What the search did right is worth stating too. Every number in this paper comes from a stamped block or a record the search wrote itself. The holdout was spent once, on a composite and a layer that were both frozen, after a declared refresh and a reproduction on the new bytes, with its expectations written before the first holdout run started. The mixed result therefore carries evidential weight: it was not chosen, and it is consistent with what the in-window beta and regime diagnostics had printed.

# XII. Limitations

The study rests on one frozen snapshot from a single vendor over a single window, so every result can be reproduced exactly. Testing the loop on other data sources and periods is the natural next step. The translations are as faithful as Sharadar’s fields allow, and where a field departs from the original definition, the choice is declared openly. Depreciation embedded in cost lines is taken as reported rather than re-estimated, operating leases enter debt from the adoption of ASC 842, and every within-sector rank uses the vendor’s current sector classification. Performance delistings are rare in this liquid universe, so the Shumway convention for delisting returns seldom applies and has little bearing on the results.

The pre-registration and the holdout each test a specific, well-defined part of the work. The rules and the construction (the family blend, the residual-only Stage 2 bar, the membership band, the within-sector ranks and the hedge) draw on what is known about how factors behaved over 1999 to 2021 and over the years reserved here as the holdout, as any design grounded in the literature does. What the pre-registration secures is the selection: every candidate was judged by rules fixed before it was screened, so no choice inside the search could be tuned to its results. The holdout tests that selection cleanly, and it is not presented as an independent test of the construction. The holdout was protected by rule rather than by withholding data. The first snapshot already contained those months, and the harness refused any in-search run that reached them, so they were used once, at the end.

Every decision in the loop is recorded together with what was known when it was made. The bars, the window, the lag, the blend, the within-sector ranks and the hedge were all fixed before the first candidate was screened. The families were defined by economic meaning after the Stage 1 results were in, but before any Stage 2 number existed. Rulings on how to apply the rules were logged as each case arose, and the expectations for the holdout were written down after the in-window results and before the holdout was opened. None of these rulings added a new rule, and when each was written, the holdout was the only data not yet seen.

The hedged headline is not a pure market-neutral return, for two reasons. The hedge uses the market’s total return rather than its excess return, which credits the short market position with the risk-free rate, and its trailing beta lagged the holdout’s realised beta. Hedging with the market excess return and constraining beta directly in the construction layer correct both, and both corrected returns are small out of sample.

Fifty-seven months is short. The holdout cannot distinguish a dead edge from a long drawdown, one year carries it, and the ex-regime diagnostic is mechanical on five calendar years.

Multiple testing is controlled only by the bar levels. Under a global null, about one Stage 1 false pass ({{null_etot}}) and about half a Stage 2 false acceptance ({{null_e3}}) are expected, against {{n_s1_pass}} passes and {{n_s2_acc}} acceptances. No family-wise or false-discovery correction was pre-registered.

In-window layer figures describe tradability only, and the cost range is wide. The two spread rows bracket spending on spread alone, the risk model omits the alpha families’ common factor and understates risk by more than a factor of two, and the layer’s gross return faded after 2002. About {{skip1_share}}% of the in-window IC arrives on the first trading day of the holding month: starting the forward return at the next month’s first trade lowered the composite IC from {{skip1_close_ic}} to {{skip1_ic}}, mostly in the value, investment and financing legs, with the momentum seed also lower, rather than in the short-horizon legs.

No per-leg or per-family holdout attribution exists, by protocol, so the holdout’s concentration and decile shape are reported without attribution. Families are judgements, declared by label, and one assignment diverged from the data. The seed legs were never screened, and four of the five would not have passed alone.

The loop has been run once, and I designed it. Its autonomy covers the execution of a search, not the choice of research question: the catalogue, the bars, the construction, the window and the phases were mine, fixed before the loop started. A second run on a new catalogue, window or vendor, set up by someone else, would be the real test of whether the framework carries its guarantees without its designer. The run also does not show how the loop behaves with weaker models; the harness would measure the same numbers, but the translation, review and interpretation would likely need more correction.

The agents made mistakes, and the record documents each one. The runner misread figures in its own summaries, let a tag command run after a commit had failed, and overstated several claims in its written reports. Each error was found by a mechanism, whether a check against the stamped blocks or the registry, the records checker or the number audit, and none by the runner noticing it itself. This is why the guarantees in this paper rest on the safeguards enforced by code.

# XIII. What Transfers

The specific composite is the least portable part of this work: the snapshot is licensed, and the composite’s out-of-sample edge, once its market exposure is removed, was small. What transfers is the framework, and none of its mechanisms depends on the data vendor or on the particular language model.

Agents propose, code decides, and a stronger model reviews. The runner ran the loop, the sub-agents did the specialised work, the advisor was consulted at the boundaries and before acceptances, and I resolved five of the seven questions in three decisions. The runner made mistakes, and every one that mattered was caught by a mechanism rather than by a person. That division of labour is the core of the design, and it is what lets a research programme run for days without supervision and still be audited afterwards.

Pointing the loop at a new problem means changing four things: the catalogue the inventory reads, the data layer and field map the translator writes against, the pre-registration file, and the standing instruction file. The stamps, the phase gates, the hooks, the permission tiers, the record and the roles of the sub-agents stay as they are. The construction used here, ranking within sector and hedging to the market, is set in the pre-registration file and touches none of them. In principle the same loop could search an international-equity or a fixed-income catalogue, or a set of machine-learning signals, under the same guarantees; this paper has shown it on one.

Provenance can be a property of the bytes rather than of anyone’s conduct. Stamp the harness, the configuration, the composite and the data on every result, and refuse results that do not match. {{n_runs_started}} runs later, the pre-registration had provably not moved, and the data had moved once, by a declared and approved refresh whose effect on every in-window number is itself recorded.

A search whose denominator is the whole catalogue, with every exclusion logged and its reason measured, cannot quietly leave the inconvenient predictors unexamined. Inventory everything, then exclude for measured reasons.

Selecting on information and reporting construction separates two questions that are usually confounded. A residual-IC bar cannot be gamed by portfolio choices, and a family blend stops near-duplicates from taking whole votes. This search also shows the bar’s limit: it admits legs whose information comes with a market exposure, and it says so in the beta rows rather than in the verdict. Printing the raw series and the beta beside every hedged number is what let the out-of-sample result be read at all.

An order fixed before scores exist, and bars read as registered, are cheap and had teeth. The ratchet’s order was declared once and decided between near-twins, and the guard’s known bias was disclosed rather than patched mid-search.

Writing the expectations for the holdout before the spend is what makes a mixed result reportable rather than a disappointment to be explained away. They were written, the block was spent once, and the result is reported against them without retuning.

The repository holds every record this paper is built from, the scripts that generate its tables and figures from them, and the commands that re-run any measurement on the snapshot. The holdout is spent; re-running it would be a second look. A new hypothesis needs a new window.

# Appendix F. The Accepted Legs, Family by Family

This appendix describes the fourteen accepted signals in Table IX, grouped by the family each one joined. Figures are the Stage 1 and Stage 2 registry rows, acceptance-time bytes.

**Profitability.** Four legs joined the Fama-French operating-profitability characteristic, the most of any family, so each holds a forty-fifth of the composite. CBOperProf, the cash-based operating profitability of Ball, Gerakos, Linnainmaa, and Nikolaev (2016), strips accruals out of operating profit and scales by assets. Its IC of {{s1ic_cboperprof}} (t-stat {{s1t_cboperprof}}) is uniform across the liquidity tiers, and its spread is a short-leg effect: it identifies cash-unprofitable firms to avoid rather than grading the winners. Financials and real estate are nearly absent by the catalogue’s own exclusion. Its residual against v1 was {{rt_cboperprof}}, half its own IC. GP, the gross profitability of Novy-Marx (2013), has the cleanest standalone record of the profitability passers: a raw Sharpe of {{rawsh1_gp}}, a hedged Sharpe without its three best years of 0.92, and a hedged annual long-short negative in only two years. It cleared at {{rt_gp}}. roaq, the quarterly return on assets of Balakrishnan, Bartov, and Faurel (2010), is a quarterly signal whose IC halves after a quarter; its residual of {{rt_roaq}} carried it against a base that already held two profitability legs. RoE, net income over book equity in the catalogue’s Haugen and Baker (1996) construction, shares its denominator with the seed leg and cleared at {{rt_roe}} with the lowest residual share of its ladder. OperProfRD was rejected in this family, its information already held.

**Investment.** PctAcc, the percent accruals of Hafzalla, Lundholm, and Van Winkle (2011), joined the asset-growth characteristic under the decision that absorbed the accruals label into investment. Its IC is modest at {{s1ic_pctacc}} but steady (t-stat {{s1t_pctacc}}), its raw spread has the best Sharpe of the passers at {{rawsh1_pctacc}}, and its beta is near zero. It was the first rung of the ratchet, at a residual t-stat of {{rt_pctacc}} and a residual share of {{share_pctacc}}, the information showing in the composite’s IC rather than in its spread (Section II.F).

**External financing.** This family was opened by ShareIss5Y, Daniel and Titman’s (2006) five-year share issuance, and filled by XFIN, the net external financing of Bradshaw, Richardson, and Sloan (2006). ShareIss5Y is the lowest-turnover signal among the passers, at under seven percent a month, with an IC nearly flat over horizons and the strongest guard statistic of the search (+{{gt_shareiss5y}}); it scores from June 2003 because of the 65-month history it needs. XFIN, the sum of net equity and net debt financing over assets, cleared the residual bar at {{rt_xfin}} while every diagnostic of its rung moved against it, the case Section VI describes. ShareIss1Y and NetEquityFinance, whose annual IC series move 0.95 with XFIN’s, were rejected as spanned.

**Value.** cfp, operating cash flow to price from Desai, Rajgopal, and Venkatachalam (2004), joined book-to-market. Its IC of {{s1ic_cfp}} (t-stat {{s1t_cfp}}) is front-loaded, 0.039 in the first half against 0.010 in the second, and two thirds of its summed hedged long-short sits in 2000, 2002 and 2021. Its stock-level residual of {{rt_cfp}} and its portfolio spanning alpha (t-stat {{cfp_span_t}}) disagree, and both are reported. CF, whose annual IC moves 0.96 with cfp’s, and NetPayoutYield were rejected in this family.

**Momentum.** TrendFactor, from Han, Zhou, and Zhu (2016), forecasts next month’s return from moving averages of price at several horizons, with the weights on those averages estimated on trailing cross-sections. It is almost uncorrelated with the composite (a spanning R² of 0.01), its standalone raw return is the largest of the passers at 10.1% a year, and its beta is near zero. Its IC one month on is close to zero, its turnover is 63% a month, and one year, 2009, carries half of its summed hedged long-short. It scores from 2003 and cleared at {{rt_trendfactor}}.

**Volatility.** MaxRet, the maximum daily return in the signal month from Bali, Cakici, and Whitelaw (2011), opened the volatility family at v7. It has one of the largest standalone ICs of the passers, {{s1ic_maxret}} (t-stat {{s1t_maxret}}), uniform across tiers, and a residual t-stat of {{rt_maxret}}. Its portfolio is weak and short the market: a raw Sharpe of {{rawsh1_maxret}}, a beta of {{beta1_maxret}}, and turnover of 71% a month. Its rung is the clearest case of the hedge reaching the guard (Section VI). IdioVol3F, the one-month idiosyncratic volatility of Ang, Hodrick, Xing, and Zhang (2006), joined it at the thinnest margin of the search, {{rt_idiovol3f}}; its beta of {{beta1_idiovol3f}} is the most negative of the accepted legs. RealizedVol and IdioVolAHT were rejected in this family.

**Short-term reversal.** STreversal, last month’s return from Jegadeesh (1990), opened its own family, a ninth of the composite. It is the month the momentum seed skips, and its scores barely correlate with momentum’s. Its IC of {{s1ic_streversal}} (t-stat {{s1t_streversal}}) lives in the small tier, it turns over 86% of its top decile a month, and its beta is positive, so the hedge turns its raw spread negative. Its residual exceeded its own IC, which triggered and passed an audit (Section VI).

**Liquidity.** zerotrade6M, the zero-trading-day and turnover measure of Liu (2006) over six months, opened the liquidity family at v12. In this universe almost no name has a zero-volume day, so the signal is in effect low turnover; its raw beta is {{beta1_zerotrade6m}}, and its annual IC moves with the volatility legs (Section XI). It cleared at {{rt_zerotrade6m}}. VolumeTrend, from Haugen and Baker (1996), is the slope of a stock’s share volume over the past five years scaled by its mean volume, with a falling trend attractive; it is a mid-tier effect with a thin IC margin, scores from 2003, and cleared at {{rt_volumetrend}}. The other two zero-trade windows and BidAskSpreadFlip were rejected in this family.

Size gained no leg; no Stage 1 passer carried the size label.
